Project 1 — Global education systems¶

Session 3: link the dataframes¶

AI Clinic · PGE1

Context¶

EducationTech wants to expand internationally and asks whether the World Bank's EdStats data can help choose countries or regions. Session 2 loaded the five files and mapped how they are linked. This session builds one clean, linked table to work on.

Objectives of the session¶

  1. Check that the indicator codes match between series and data.
  2. Keep only the countries in data, so that the same inhabitants are not counted several times.
  3. Find the best-filled recent year(s), to decide which period to work on.
  4. Propose and plot relevant charts (filled cells per year, countries per region…).

Two decisions taken with the teacher and the group frame this notebook:

  • Period: the teacher asked for three years; we work on 2012–2014 (section 3 checks that this is the right choice).
  • Business hypothesis: the brief does not say what EducationTech sells. We assume a paid online learning platform for upper-secondary and university students. This hypothesis drives the choice of indicators (section 5).

Only data, country and series are needed here. The two note files (country_series, footnote) are not used in this session.

The rule "no Region = aggregate" is almost right, with three exceptions:

  • Gibraltar (GIB) and Nauru (NRU) have no region, yet they are real territories;
  • British Virgin Islands (VGB) is in data but has no row in country at all.

So the aggregates are the rows without a region, except Gibraltar and Nauru. The three territories are given their World Bank region by hand, so that every country has one.

Decision: work on 2012–2014, taking for each country and indicator its most recent value in those three years.

  • The teacher asked for three years. 2012–2014 are the three best-filled recent years once the five-yearly Barro-Lee effect is removed: 2012 is the best, 2013 close behind, 2014 still usable. 2015 already loses a third of the education data.
  • Taking the most recent value over three years rather than a single year more than doubles the coverage (23.5 % of pairs instead of 10.7 % for 2015 alone).
  • Two consequences to keep in mind: values do not all date from the same year (the year is kept for each value), and the Barro-Lee attainment data has no value at all in 2012–2014, so the education level of adults cannot be used.

5. Which indicators to keep?¶

5.1 The rule¶

An indicator is kept if it passes two filters:

  1. Relevance for our hypothesis (paid online learning for upper-secondary and university students): it says something about the size of the market, the educational demand, the access to an online product or the ability to pay.
  2. Coverage: at least one value in 2012–2014 for at least 60 % of the 217 countries (130 countries). Below that, comparisons between regions are no longer meaningful.

5.2 Coverage of every indicator in 2012–2014¶

Why these twelve.

  • Market size — how many potential customers. Total population for scale, the 15–24 age group and the populations of upper-secondary and tertiary age for the target itself, growth to know whether the market is expanding.
  • Education demand — are these young people actually in school? Ratios and numbers are both needed: a 90 % ratio in Luxembourg and a 60 % ratio in India say nothing about the size of the market.
  • Access — an online product needs internet. This is the only connectivity indicator still filled after 2009.
  • Ability to pay — GDP per capita in purchasing power parity, because 1,000 dollars do not buy the same thing everywhere; and the share of students in private institutions, the best available proxy for families' willingness to pay for education. It is also the weakest indicator of the list (137 countries).

The income group of each country (from country) is kept alongside: it is not an indicator, but it is useful to split results.

Objective Result
